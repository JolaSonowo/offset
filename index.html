Tax-loss harvesting

Sell an investment that’s down, claim the loss, and buy something similar straight away so you stay in the market.

  • 01Your losses cancel out gains you made elsewhere. With no gains, they reduce up to $3,000 of your income a year, and the rest carries forward.
  • 02It mostly delays tax. Your replacement has a lower cost, so you owe more when you eventually sell. Meanwhile, the tax you didn’t pay keeps growing.
  • 03It saves tax for good if the losses cancel short-term gains (taxed like income) and you later pay the lower long-term rate, or if you never sell: heirs get a fresh cost basis, and donated shares aren’t taxed.

The wash-sale rule

Buy the same or a “substantially identical” investment within 30 days either side of the sale, and the loss doesn’t count. That includes your IRA and your spouse’s accounts.

Direct indexing

Own the stocks in an index yourself instead of the fund. Even when the index is up, plenty of individual stocks are down, and each one is a loss you can harvest.

  • 01Far more losses to harvest than with funds, especially in the first few years.
  • 02Leave out stocks you don’t want, like your employer’s, and donate your most appreciated shares.
  • 03Costs about 0.2–0.4% a year versus about 0.03% for an index fund, and the losses dry up as your stocks rise over time.

Long-short strategies

Direct indexing with extra borrowed exposure. In a 130/30 strategy, every $100 you invest becomes $130 of stocks owned and $30 of other stocks sold short.

  • 01A short position loses money when its stock rises, and that loss can be harvested too, so losses keep coming instead of drying up.
  • 02Mostly used by people with big gains to offset, such as from selling a business or a large stock position. Minimums are often $1 million or more.
  • 03Higher fees and borrowing costs, bigger swings from the index, and short positions can lose without limit. The tax rules are more complex too.

How the simulation works
  • Each market moves week by week. Every stock moves with the market, with its sector and on its own, including the occasional big surprise.
  • The index fund just holds. Direct indexing starts with the same 100 stocks, sells any that drop past your threshold, and holds a similar sector fund for at least 31 days before switching back.
  • Long-short owns most of the stocks at 1.3× and shorts a separate group at 0.3×. It harvests both and rebalances every quarter, picking the sales that cost the least tax.
  • Gains and losses are short- or long-term by how long each was held, netted at year end like the IRS does, with unused losses carried forward. Tax saved is reinvested in the index fund.
  • Your bracket sets the rates: short-term gains at your bracket, long-term at 0%, 15% or 20%, plus the 3.8% investment income tax from the 32% bracket up, plus any state tax.
  • Not included: dividends, trading costs, stock-picking skill and real-world limits on borrowing shares.

Is it worth it for you?

Worth a closer look if you

  • have big gains to offset, especially short-term ones
  • are in a high tax bracket or a high-tax state
  • plan to hold for life or give shares to charity
  • keep adding money over time

Probably not if you

  • invest mostly in an IRA or 401(k), where harvesting doesn’t apply
  • are in a low bracket, where long-term gains can be taxed at 0%
  • would pay more in extra fees than you’d save in tax
  • will sell everything soon at the same tax rate
